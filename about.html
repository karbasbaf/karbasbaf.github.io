About

Strings to Stars

Mohammad Mahdi Karbasbaf, in studio

I build instruments by hand. I have spent years inside the walls of a 17th-century palace, listening for what its architecture was designed to do to sound. I write in scripts that take a decade to steady the hand for. And now I design book covers — because all four of these practices are, underneath, the same discipline: finding the hidden structure inside something beautiful.

My work began with the Tār and the Setār — traditional Persian string instruments I learned to build and restore by hand, long before I had language for why their proportions mattered. Building an instrument teaches you that beauty and function are not separate decisions; the curve of a soundboard is also its acoustic behavior. That lesson followed me into research.

At Ali Qapu Palace in Isfahan — a UNESCO World Heritage site — I studied how its 17th-century architecture shapes sound, work that became peer-reviewed research published in Elsevier's Applied Acoustics and Digital Applications in Archaeology and Cultural Heritage. Palace walls, it turns out, were acoustic instruments too; no one had simply asked them the right questions yet.

Calligraphy runs alongside all of this. I practice Nastaliq and Thuluth — Persian and Arabic script traditions that demand years of disciplined repetition before a single letterform is trustworthy. Every calligraphic element I produce is original, hand-written artwork, never a digital font — because a font can reproduce a letterform's shape, but not the physical decision made in the moment of writing it.

Book cover design is where these threads meet. A cover for a book on Persian history or Sufi poetry needs more than a striking image — it needs to be made by someone who has actually stood inside the tradition it represents. That is the work I now do for international publishers: covers built from research, calligraphy, and a decade of hand-practiced discipline, not from stock imagery or borrowed pattern.
