A Biography in Resonance

A lute, a palace, a nebula — across each, M.M. Karbasbaf has asked the same question, and has yet to finish asking it: where does a wave decide to speak, and what shape is it asking to be given?

I

The Instrument — 2014–2020

The inquiry began as a disposition of mind: the popular image of outer space as an absolute, silent vacuum is itself a simplification. Even the "vacuum" of quantum physics is never truly empty, and astronomers have already recorded genuine pressure waves moving through the thin plasma that fills intracluster space. If that premise holds, what follows for how sound behaves across such varying degrees of near-vacuum, from interstellar gas to the plasma deep inside a nebula? And is the shorthand "sound cannot travel through space" a physical law, or a convenient approximation that holds only until the medium is examined closely enough?

The scientific method is only the most advanced language humanity has yet found for interpreting nature, not its final word. It is self-evident that humanity must, at some point, move toward a deeper understanding of resonance at this scale, and through it, a deeper understanding of the cosmos. The more pressing question is this: why should that person not be us, and why should that moment not be now?

This line of thought had to begin somewhere, and its center was resonance. So began a journey through the phenomenology of resonance across different media, a journey whose origin can be traced to a workshop in 2014, while repairing an antique oud.

That same inquiry, deepened by close study of the designs and structures of Italian violins, Giuseppe Guarneri del Gesù's instruments above all, led to the invention and development of the Golden Method, published in 2017 and offered to the scientific community.

This question had already found its first formal shape in a mechanical engineering thesis on opera halls as acoustic resonators. From 2015 onward, building and repairing Tār and Setār, traditional lute-family stringed instruments of Persia, meant testing that same question, first by hand, and only later by measurement.

The Golden Method, built from Fibonacci ratios for determining the size and placement of the soundhole across the lute family and other oval-bodied instruments, can since serve as one practical reference for luthiers and musical-acoustics researchers in selecting soundhole coordinates suited to controlling the axial vibration of an instrument's soundboard. An instrument named Mahavaa was built on this method as well, and the 2017 paper (Arts, 2017) was written and published on the basis of that construction.

A second study (Applied Acoustics, 2024) traced how the soundholes of short-necked lute-family instruments developed and evolved across history, from antiquity to the present, worldwide, ultimately demonstrating that their most evolved configuration, the triple-hole design, a solution long assumed to be merely decorative, outperforms the single-hole design in loudness and resonance. In both cases, the craftsman's inherited instinct for proportion had already solved the problem the mathematics was only now catching up to.

II

The Palace — 2019–2025

The same question, asked of stone instead of wood, led to Isfahan. Inside the seventeenth-century Music Hall of Ali Qapu Palace, a UNESCO World Heritage site built in 1597, the ornamental niches known as Tong Bori turned out, under analysis, to function as precisely tuned Helmholtz resonators, shaping reverberation with the same intent a luthier brings to a soundhole (Digital Applications in Archaeology and Cultural Heritage, 2025). Because the building could not be touched, the methodology had to be built, and extended, around that constraint: small-scale physical modeling paired with in-situ acoustic measurement, together establishing a rigorous, entirely non-invasive approach, one transferable to heritage buildings anywhere, for acoustic researchers and archaeologists alike.

The same resonator logic, confirmed inside a four-hundred-year-old palace, was then rebuilt for the present: an array of two hundred spherical resonators, engineered and tested as a zero-energy acoustic treatment for net-zero buildings (Next Research, 2026). A further study (Journal of Sound and Vibration, 2026) extends the inquiry to the Kar-bandi family of Persian ornamental patterns, among them the muqarnas vault, asking the same question of forms still assumed, almost everywhere, to be purely decorative, and finding in their geometry evidence that Persian architects understood acoustic control of interior space not as an afterthought but as integral to a building's very nature.

III

The Nebula — 2024–Present

The same question is now being asked at a scale no workbench or palace could hold. Current research, still in preparation, investigates whether the resonance governing a Herbig-Haro object, gas set oscillating by a young star's outflow deep inside a distant nebula, can be described in the same analytical language built for a soundhole and a Persian niche, through electromagnetic and plasma mechanisms rather than mechanical ones. The tools have changed: Python, Astropy, computational wave modeling. The question hasn't. Given a cavity, a boundary, a medium capable of oscillation, what shape does the wave ask to be given?

IV

A Guiding Commitment

Behind every one of these studies lies one shared commitment: to build methods that can step outside the laboratory. Methods that any luthier, any architect, any researcher, working with modest means and without costly technical complexity, can reproduce, test, and carry forward across generations. This is what might be called humanizing acoustics: making its understanding, its development, and its use something within reach of every person, at every scale, with no wall of technical privilege standing between them and the science. Perhaps this, too, is an echo of a quality Persian architecture has long carried in its own form: a humaneness that never places itself out of reach.

V

A Parallel Craft

Alongside the research, one line of craft has continued without interruption. Thuluth calligraphy — practiced in the Persian tradition of the Timurid and Safavid courts as well as the Ottoman Turkish style — pursued to a professional standard. Two short films, Womanly Temple (International Short Film Festival Oberhausen, Germany, 2016) and A Short Film About Evolution (Farhang Film Festival, USA, 2014). And, more recently, new covers for canonical works of literature and philosophy. Different instruments, same instinct: an attention to proportion, restraint, and the exact weight a form is asked to carry.

The universe resonates: we simply need to learn how to listen, at every scale.

— M.M. Karbasbaf
